Oolaa Smart Mirrors at Eight Star Street, Hong Kong

Mar 4, 2024

At Eight Star Street by Swire in Hong Kong, Oolaa smart mirrors do considerably more than reflect. Equipped with advanced technology and a full range of functions and applications, they connect to the home network to enable smarter operations and services — serving, first and foremost, as the control center for the connected home. It is a telling choice by a developer with a reputation to protect: the mirror residents already use every morning becomes the one surface that runs the home.

The mirror as the home's control center

Every connected home eventually faces the same question: where does the household actually operate it? Wall panels get buried behind furniture. Phone apps get buried under notifications. The bathroom mirror has neither problem — it is looked at, every day, by every resident, at the two moments that bookend the day. Making it the control center is not a gimmick; it is ergonomics.

From the mirror, residents can run the scenes that matter: lighting, climate, curtains, and routines, all from the surface they are already facing. Underneath, Oolaa supports the full spread of smart-home protocols — Matter, Zigbee, Bluetooth, Wi-Fi, and KNX — and works through Matter with Apple Home, Google Home, Alexa, and Xiaomi. In practice that means the mirror does not ask the household to standardize on one ecosystem before move-in day; it meets whatever the residents already own. Oolaa's whole-home approach is one reason developers keep specifying the brand.

What “control center” means on an ordinary Tuesday

Picture the day rather than the technology. Morning: the mirror shows the weather and the day's schedule while the bathroom warms to the preferred scene — no phone in wet hands required, because voice control in Cantonese, Mandarin, or English is built in via Google Assistant. Leaving: one command sets the away scene — lights, climate, and standby loads handled together. Evening: the wind-down scene dims the home while tomorrow's calendar waits on the glass.

None of this demands a technically minded resident. That is the point developers care about: the interface was designed by Oolaa's in-house team for the mirror as a medium — large, calm, glanceable — so a grandparent and a teenager operate it with equal confidence. Technology that needs a manual is a support cost; technology that needs no manual is an amenity.

Why developers specify the smart mirror

For a developer, a smart mirror solves three problems at once. It differentiates the unit in a competitive luxury market with something buyers can see and touch at the viewing — unlike wiring hidden in walls. It consolidates functions: one beautiful surface replaces a cluster of switches, panels, and thermostats, which matters in Hong Kong bathrooms where every centimetre is contested. And it is a single accountable supplier: Oolaa is a Hong Kong company with its own factory in Shenzhen, supplying and supporting its products directly rather than through trading layers.

That direct accountability shows up after handover, when residents actually need help. With 31,000+ installations across Hong Kong and Macau — including projects for Sun Hung Kai — Oolaa brings a service record most smart-home brands cannot, because most smart-home brands do not make their own hardware. Factory-direct means the company that answers the phone is the company that built the mirror.

Under the glass

What residents experience as simplicity rests on several deliberate decisions. The display and mirror optics are balanced so the surface reads as a true mirror first and a screen second. The interface is Oolaa's own — not a skinned tablet OS — which is why casting a phone to the mirror needs no login: “No Login. Just Cast.” Voice runs through Google Assistant, including Cantonese, so the household speaks its own language to its own home. And the bathroom-grade build — IP65 waterproofing, defogger, motion detection — means the control center keeps working in the room where electronics usually go to die.

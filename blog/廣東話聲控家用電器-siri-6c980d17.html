Cantonese Voice Control of Home Appliances with Siri

Apr 30, 2023

If the only advantage of a smart home is controlling appliances with your phone, you might ask: why must I unlock my phone and hunt for an app just to switch on a light? That kind of operation makes smart home systems less convenient, not more. And in Hong Kong, adoption faced a second barrier: you could not command your appliances in your mother tongue. Only English or Mandarin voice commands were understood — which felt impersonal and alienating. Today, at last, there is a way to control home appliances in Cantonese, including through Siri. It may benefit the people, and it may finally move the Hong Kong smart home market forward.

The language gap that held Hong Kong smart homes back

Hong Kong's smart home market grew more slowly than its technology deserved, and language was one of the reasons. The voice assistants that make smart homes feel effortless elsewhere arrived speaking English and Mandarin — but not Cantonese, the language of the Hong Kong home. Asking a family to issue commands in a second language, with unfamiliar phrasing, reintroduces exactly the friction voice control was meant to remove. It feels unnatural, and unnatural technology does not get used.

The gap was not for lack of asking. Discussing this technology meant going back and forth with Hong Kong's leading tech companies, whose answers were often unclear; asking product managers at mainland tech firms, whose answers were not always certain; and even the manufacturers themselves did not know whether Cantonese support could succeed. Cantonese is, in the global technology market, a niche dialect — commercially small, linguistically complex with its nine tones, and easy for big platforms to deprioritise. That is precisely why a working Cantonese solution matters so much: it serves the people the global market overlooked.

Why mother-tongue control changes everything

Voice control only feels like the future when it understands how you actually speak. In a Hong Kong household, commands fly in Cantonese — quick, tonal, colloquial. A system that forces you to translate your own thoughts into English before speaking is not really voice control; it is a language test with a light switch at the end.

The difference is most dramatic across generations. Elderly parents and grandparents who have spoken Cantonese all their lives can command appliances naturally, without learning foreign-language phrases or navigating phone apps. Children pick it up instantly. When every generation in the home can use the system in their mother tongue, the smart home stops being one person's hobby and becomes the family's infrastructure. That inclusiveness is what turns adoption from a trickle into a movement — and a movement is what the Hong Kong market has been waiting for.

How Cantonese voice control works in practice

Today there are real paths to Cantonese appliance control. Siri supports Cantonese voice commands for HomeKit-compatible devices, so iPhone users can speak naturally to control lights, switches and scenes. Oolaa's smart mirrors, meanwhile, support voice control through Google Assistant with Cantonese supported — turning the mirror you stand in front of every morning into a Cantonese-speaking interface for the home.

Underneath the voice layer, open protocols do the heavy lifting. Oolaa's smart-home products support Matter, Zigbee, Bluetooth, Wi-Fi and KNX, so a spoken Cantonese command can reach whatever device it needs to, and through Matter the system interoperates with Apple Home, Google Home, Alexa and Xiaomi. The foyer mirror can even serve as a whole-home control hub via Google Assistant, Bluetooth and Zigbee — one fixed point where Cantonese commands fan out to every room. The lesson for anyone planning a smart home: choose the voice interface your family will actually use, then make sure the devices underneath speak open standards.

What this means for Hong Kong's smart home future

The arrival of working Cantonese voice control removes the two objections that held the market back: the phone-app friction, and the language barrier.

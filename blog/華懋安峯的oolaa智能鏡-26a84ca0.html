Chinachem Anderson Rd Lobbies Get Smart Mirrors

June 07, 2023

At a Chinachem residential development on Anderson Road, smart-city living has arrived at the lift lobby. A 43-inch Oolaa Smart Mirror has been installed in the lift lobby of every tower — delivering real-time information to residents, and letting them control the smart-home system by voice in Cantonese. It is a glimpse of how Hong Kong's residential developments are turning shared spaces into intelligent ones, and everyday routines into something simpler and faster.

The lift lobby as an information hub

The lift lobby is the most democratic space in a residential tower. Every resident passes through it at least twice a day; no other location in the building gets that kind of guaranteed foot traffic. Yet in most developments, the lobby's walls carry nothing more informative than a notice board with curling paper announcements.

The 43-inch smart mirror changes that equation completely. At that size, the display is readable from across the lobby — large enough for real-time information that matters to everyone: weather, traffic, building announcements, management notices, and community news. Because the unit is a true mirror when the display is idle, it also serves the lobby's traditional function: a last check of appearance before heading out, now with the day's information layered alongside the reflection.

For property management, a network of lobby displays — one per tower — is a communication channel with total coverage. An urgent notice about lift maintenance, a typhoon arrangement, or a facilities booking update reaches every resident without anyone needing to check an app or read a paper notice. In a large multi-tower development, that reliability is operationally valuable, not just decorative.

Cantonese voice control for everyone

The standout feature for residents is voice control in Cantonese. Through Google Assistant integration, residents can speak to the lobby mirror in their mother tongue to interact with the smart-home system — checking information, controlling connected devices, and getting answers without touching a screen or navigating menus.

This matters enormously in Hong Kong. A significant share of residents — particularly older residents — are far more comfortable speaking Cantonese than typing on a touchscreen or navigating an English-language app. Voice in the local language collapses the technology barrier completely: if you can ask, you can use it. A grandmother who would never open a smart-home app can ask the lobby mirror a question in Cantonese and get an answer.

The choice of the lift lobby as the voice interface location is equally deliberate. It is a shared, supervised, public space — nobody needs to own a device, install anything, or create an account. The intelligence belongs to the building and serves everyone in it equally, which is exactly what "smart city" infrastructure should mean at the residential level.

Smart-city living, made simple

The development's smart-home system extends the intelligence beyond the lobby. Residents get the simple, easy-to-use smart-city lifestyle the project promises: connected living that makes daily life more convenient rather than more complicated. The lobby mirror is the visible face of that system — the place where the building's intelligence introduces itself.

Consider the resident's day. Morning: glance at the lobby mirror for weather and traffic while waiting for the lift. Midday: a building notice about weekend facilities maintenance is already on the display — no missed paper notice. Evening: ask in Cantonese about tomorrow's arrangements on the way home. None of these interactions requires a smartphone, an app, or any technical confidence. That is the real test of smart-city technology: it must serve the least technical resident as well as the most.

Oolaa's broader smart-home capabilities sit behind this simplicity — support for Matter, Zigbee, Bluetooth, Wi-Fi, and KNX protocols, interoperating with Apple Home, Google Home, Alexa, and Xiaomi ecosystems. The resident never sees any of that complexity. They see a mirror that answers when spoken to, in the language they speak at home.

Specifying lobby displays for residential towers

For developers and property managers considering intelligent lift lobbies, this project offers a practical template:

  • Size for the space. A 43-inch display is readable across a lobby; smaller screens get ignored. Match the display to the viewing distance.
  • One per tower, minimum. Coverage matters — every resident should pass one daily. Standardise the unit across towers for consistent management and spares.
  • Insist on the local language. Cantonese voice control is what makes the system usable by all residents, not just the tech-confident.
  • Keep it zero-friction. A shared lobby display must work with no accounts, no apps, and no training — walk up and use it.
  • Buy factory-direct at scale. Equipping every lift lobby in a multi-tower development is a volume project. Oolaa's own Shenzhen factory and 31,000+ Hong Kong and Macau installations make that scale routine.

Cook with Your Kitchen Assistant: Cantonese Voice

May 23, 2020

Have you imagined cooking with a smart home? A few years ago, the honest answer for most of us was no — the kitchen was where technology went to get splashed, steamed and ignored. Never say never. Oolaa's tech team developed Cantonese voice command, and kept improving it, until the dream came true: a kitchen you can talk to, in the language Hong Kong actually cooks in.

The idea sounds playful until you picture the moment it serves. Your hands are in the marinade. The wok is at the temperature where thirty seconds matter. The recipe is on your phone, which is across the room, waking up a passcode screen. Every cook knows this exact frustration, because cooking is the one household activity that fully occupies both hands — and punishes you for taking either of them away.

Why voice belongs in the kitchen

Voice is the only input that costs the cook nothing. No hands to wash before touching a screen, no gloves to remove, no phone to smear with oil. Ask, and it is done. The classic case is the countdown: a timer set by voice, mid-stir, without breaking rhythm — the difference between a cook who manages the kitchen and a kitchen that manages the cook.

Around that core, the kitchen becomes a multimedia room in its own right. A kitchen TV or smart mirror carries the recipe video, the news, the playlist — companions for the long, pleasant work of a family dinner, and instruction for the dishes you are still learning. The kitchen has always been the social center of the Hong Kong home; giving it a voice simply lets it answer back.

Cantonese, because that is how Hong Kong cooks

The language is not a detail. Instructions shouted across a kitchen — more heat, less time, check the rice — are given in Cantonese in Hong Kong homes, and a voice system that demands Mandarin or English inserts a translation step into the one room that has no time for it. Oolaa's tech team built Cantonese voice command for exactly this reason, and improved it continuously: the test of a kitchen assistant is not a quiet showroom, but a noisy family kitchen at full steam.

That continuous improvement mattered, because Cantonese is not a language that forgives lazy recognition. Tones carry meaning; kitchen Cantonese is fast, colloquial and shouted over range hoods and running water. Each round of improvement — better recognition in noise, better handling of the way families actually phrase requests — widened the circle of people the system served, from the enthusiast who installed it to the grandparent who would never have touched a settings menu but talks to the kitchen as naturally as to anyone in it.

Today, voice control across Oolaa's smart mirrors runs through Google Assistant with Cantonese support — the mature form of the work this post announced. What began as a kitchen experiment became a house-wide expectation: speak naturally, in your own language, and the home responds.

So — have you imagined cooking with your kitchen assistant? In more and more Hong Kong kitchens, the answer no longer needs imagining. The countdown is set by a sentence, the recipe is playing where the cook can see it, the music is on, and dinner is on its way. What sounded like a party trick a few years ago has become the most natural thing in the world: a cook, talking to the kitchen, in Cantonese.

Making any kitchen a little more voice-friendly

Even before the technology is chosen, a few habits and decisions make a kitchen ready for its assistant. Put the display where the cook actually stands — at the preparation counter, in the natural sightline — rather than where the cabinet plan had a gap. Build the countdown into the cooking itself: pasta water, marinades, roasts and steamed fish all run on minutes that a voice timer tracks better than a cook glancing at a clock with floury hands. Keep the entertainment close, too; a recipe video or a playlist at the counter changes the emotional register of cooking from chore to occupation. And choose the language your household thinks in. Technology adopted by the whole family survives; technology that serves only its most technical member quietly becomes furniture.
